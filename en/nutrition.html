Nutrition & Diet — The Metabolic Foundation of Wound Healing

Core concept: Nutrition is the metabolic foundation of wound healing. Inadequate calories, protein deficiency, or micronutrient imbalance directly delays healing and increases the risk of infection and complications. Patients with chronic wounds should undergo routine nutritional screening, with calories and protein requirements individually calculated by population (general, geriatric, burn).

1. How to Assess Nutritional Status

Nutritional assessment is not simply checking body weight; it is a comprehensive judgment integrating screening tools, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory data. For patients with chronic wounds, nutritional screening is recommended at initial presentation and at regular follow-up visits to detect correctable nutritional deficits early.

2. Indicators and Staging of Malnutrition

In 2019, the GLIM (Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition) proposed an international two-step diagnostic consensus, integrating previous ESPEN and ASPEN definitions to provide a cross-institutional framework for malnutrition diagnosis.

2.1 GLIM Two-Step Diagnostic Process

Step 1: First use a screening tool (SGA, MNA, MUST, etc.) to confirm "at risk of malnutrition."
Step 2: Confirm the diagnosis using GLIM criteria — at least 1 phenotypic criterion plus 1 etiologic criterion.
GLIM Diagnostic Criteria
Phenotypic CriteriaEtiologic Criteria
① Non-volitional weight loss
② Low BMI (Asia <18.5; general <20)
③ Reduced muscle mass (MM loss)		① Reduced food intake or assimilation
② Disease burden / inflammatory response (acute, chronic infection or inflammation)
GLIM: Cederholm T, et al. Clin Nutr 2019;38(1):1–11; J Nutr Gerontol Geriatr 2019 (ESPEN/ASPEN consensus).

2.2 Severity Grading

StagePhenotypic Criteria (any one)Clinical Significance
Moderate5–10% weight loss in 1–3 months; or BMI <20 (Asia <18.5)Requires nutritional intervention and close follow-up
Severe>10% weight loss in 1–3 months; or BMI <18.5 (Asia <16)Requires aggressive nutritional support; enteral or parenteral nutrition if necessary

Staging should incorporate muscle mass assessment; low BMI combined with reduced muscle mass carries higher risk.

GLIM severity grading: Cederholm T, et al. Clin Nutr 2019;38(1):1–11.

3. How to Improve Nutritional Status in Chronic Wound Patients

Patients with chronic wounds are often in a state of chronic inflammation and catabolic metabolism; simply "eating more" is frequently insufficient. Structured interventions based on individualized calorie, protein, and micronutrient requirements are needed, combined with feeding strategies to improve adherence.

3.1 Calorie Requirements

General chronic wounds30–35 kcal/kg/dayCalculated using actual or ideal body weight

Obese patientsUse adjusted body weightAdjusted weight = ideal body weight + 0.25 × (actual weight − ideal weight)
Pressure injury nutrition: Posthauer ME, et al. Adv Skin Wound Care 2022 (NPUAP clinical guideline). ESPEN geriatric: Volkert D, et al. Clin Nutr 2019.

3.4 Feeding Strategies

  • Small frequent meals: 5–6 meals per day with smaller portions; improves total intake in patients with poor appetite
  • Oral nutritional supplements (ONS): Liquid clinical nutrition formulas add calories and protein beyond regular meals; studies show ONS reduces pressure injury incidence in high-risk patients
  • Texture modification: Adjust food consistency and thickness for those with chewing or swallowing difficulties; refer to speech therapy or swallowing assessment when needed
  • Individualized menus: Design around culture, religion, allergies, and dietary preferences; avoid generic "nutrition formulas" that disregard patient acceptance
  • Multidisciplinary collaboration: Dietitians, nurses, and attending physicians jointly track intake records and weight trends
Feeding strategies and ONS: ESPEN geriatric guidelines (Volkert 2019); NPUAP pressure injury clinical practice guideline (Posthauer 2022).

4. Calorie Calculation Formulas for Three Major Populations

Basal metabolic rate (BMR) and total energy expenditure (TEE) estimation differ across populations. The following formulas serve as clinical estimation references; actual delivery should be adjusted and monitored based on individual clinical status.

FormulaTarget PopulationFormula
Harris-BenedictGeneral hospitalized patientsMale: BMR = 66.47 + 13.75 × kg + 5.0 × cm − 6.76 × age
Female: BMR = 655.1 + 9.56 × kg + 1.85 × cm − 4.68 × age
TEE = BMR × Activity Factor (AF) × Stress Factor (SF)
Mifflin-St JeorElderlyMale: BMR = 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age + 5
Female: BMR = 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age − 161
CurreriBurn adultsCalories = 25 × kg + 40 × TBSA% (TBSA capped at 50%)
GalvestonBurn childrenCalories = 1800 × BSA + 1300 × burn_area (m²)

4.2 Clinical Reminders

  • Edematous patients: Use dry weight or usual weight for calculations; avoid overestimating calorie needs based on edematous weight
  • Obese patients: Use adjusted body weight rather than actual weight to avoid overfeeding
  • Activity factor (AF): Bedridden 1.2, bedside activity 1.25–1.3, general activity 1.3–1.5; add stress factor postoperatively or for infection by severity
  • Stress factor (SF): Minor surgery 1.1–1.2; severe infection 1.2–1.4; burns 1.5–2.0 (depending on TBSA)
  • Follow-up: Formulas are only starting estimates; adjust continuously based on weight change, clinical response, and laboratory data
⚠️ Overfeeding risk: In bedridden or critically ill patients, excessive calorie delivery may cause hyperglycemia, fatty liver, and increased respiratory burden; reach targets gradually and monitor tolerance.
Burn nutrition clinical guidelines: Walker H, et al. Burns 2023 (ISBI practice guidelines for burn care). ESPEN geriatric: Volkert D, et al. Clin Nutr 2019.

The nutritional plan for chronic wound patients should be integrated with the wound care team, with regular reassessment of weight, protein markers, and wound progress.

6. Clinical Diet Design

Clinical diet design translates calculated calorie and protein targets into actionable meal plans tailored to each population. The goal is not only meeting requirements on paper but also ensuring patients can actually consume the prescribed nutrition — accounting for appetite, dentition, swallowing capacity, and disease-specific metabolic demands.

6.1 Population-Specific Diet Recommendations

PopulationDiet Design PrinciplesKey Food ChoicesFeeding Strategy
General inpatientBalanced nutrition for steady recovery; progressive diet texture: clear liquid → liquid → soft → regular as toleratedHigh-quality protein: chicken breast, sea bass (perch), tofu, eggs; plant-oil cooking: steaming, boiling, light stir-fry; snack supplements: milk, yogurtRegular 3 meals; add snacks if intake inadequate; advance diet texture per GI tolerance and postoperative recovery
ElderlyCombat sarcopenia with high nutrient density; small frequent meals; texture adjustment for dentition and swallowing: finely chopped vegetables, ground meat, stewed meat, steamed eggKey protein distribution: spread evenly across 3 meals + snacks (≥25–30 g protein per meal); Omega-3 fats: mackerel, salmon, ground nuts5–6 meals/day; supplement with geriatric nutritional formula if oral intake insufficient
BurnExtremely high calorie, ultra-high protein, strict fat control to meet hypermetabolic demands; carbohydrate-based: rice, noodles, sweet potato, oatsProtein maximized: meat/fish/egg/dairy/beans + glutamine/arginine/HMB supplements; strict fat restriction: no fried food, no bacon, no heavy thickening; Omega-3 fish oil or flaxseed oilFeed every 2–3 hours; burn-specific medical nutrition formula; may require parenteral nutrition (PN) if enteral intake insufficient
⚠️ Texture progression: In postoperative or critically ill patients, advance diet texture only after confirming GI tolerance (no nausea, vomiting, distention). Rapid progression may cause feeding intolerance; when in doubt, maintain current texture and reassess in 24–48 hours.

7. Clinical Feeding Routes

The golden rule of clinical nutrition is: "If the gut works, use it." Enteral feeding — whether oral, nasogastric (NG), or post-pyloric — preserves gut mucosal integrity, maintains intestinal immune function, and reduces infectious complications compared with parenteral nutrition (PN). Parenteral nutrition is reserved for patients with gut failure, intolerable ileus, or when enteral delivery cannot meet requirements despite maximal effort.

7.1 Feeding Route Comparison by Population

PopulationFirst choiceSecond-line / stepwiseKey clinical consideration
General inpatientOral (PO) with texture adjustmentNG tube (if intake <50% of requirements for 3–5 consecutive days)Restore autonomous eating ASAP; maintain GI motility; avoid prolonged unnecessary tube feeding
ElderlyOral (PO) with extreme caution for aspiration; texture modificationNG tube or PEG (if severe dysphagia, recurrent aspiration pneumonia)Focus on preventing aspiration pneumonia and improving quality of life; PEG preferred for long-term feeding
BurnEarly enteral nutrition (EN) via NG or nasoduodenal tube within 24 h of injuryOral + PN (when ileus in large burns; IV supplements fill the gap)Ultra-high calorie needs cannot be met orally; early feeding prevents bacterial translocation and sepsis

7.2 Route Selection Details

  • General inpatient: Oral intake is always the first choice; it preserves eating autonomy, stimulates GI motility, and carries the lowest infection risk. If oral intake falls below 50% of estimated requirements for 3–5 consecutive days, escalate to NG feeding. Assess swallowing and dentition early; involve speech therapy and dietitians for texture adjustment. Restore autonomous oral eating as soon as clinical status permits — prolonged unnecessary tube feeding delays recovery and increases discomfort.
  • Elderly: Oral feeding remains preferred but requires vigilant aspiration assessment — evaluate cough, gag reflex, voice changes, and any history of aspiration pneumonia. Texture-modified diets (soft, minced, pureed, thickened liquids) reduce aspiration risk. When oral routes fail or recurrent aspiration occurs, NG feeding is the short-term option; for long-term needs (≥4 weeks) or when NG is poorly tolerated, PEG (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy) is preferred for its better tolerability, lower complication rate, and quality-of-life benefits. Priority is preventing aspiration pneumonia — the leading cause of malnutrition-related mortality in elderly patients.
  • Burn: Early enteral nutrition within 24 hours of injury is a standard of care in major burns — it maintains gut mucosal integrity, reduces bacterial translocation and sepsis risk, and attenuates the hypermetabolic response. Nasogastric or nasoduodenal tubes are first-line because oral intake alone almost never meets the extreme calorie and protein demands of large burns (TBSA >20%). When ileus or GI intolerance prevents adequate enteral delivery, parenteral nutrition supplements fill the gap — but enteral feeding should continue at tolerance level and never be fully abandoned. "If the gut works, use it" — even minimal enteral feeding is better than none.
⚠️ Aspiration prevention in elderly: Bedside screening (cough on water sip, voice quality) is mandatory before any oral feeding in elderly patients with neurological impairment or history of aspiration. When in doubt, keep NPO and refer for formal swallowing assessment (FEES or VFSS).
ESPEN/ASPEN consensus on enteral and parenteral nutrition; ISBI burn practice guidelines (Walker H, et al. Burns 2023;49(3):577–601); Volkert D, et al. Clin Nutr 2019 (ESPEN geriatric guidelines on dysphagia and PEG).
